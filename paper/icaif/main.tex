\begin{abstract}
Diffusion and score-based generative models have, within a few years, spread
across nearly every quantitative-finance task: synthetic data generation, limit-
order-book (LOB) simulation, risk and scenario generation, derivative pricing and
hedging, and portfolio construction. Their appeal is structural---stable
likelihood-based training, strong mode coverage, native conditioning, and a
stochastic-differential-equation (SDE) formulation that sits naturally beside the
It\^o calculus and stochastic control finance already uses. We present, to our
knowledge, the first survey dedicated to diffusion models \emph{in finance}. We
organize a fast-growing and fragmented literature along two axes---methodological
family and financial task---consolidate how diffusion's conditioning and SDE
structure are exploited for finance-specific goals (risk-neutral generation,
stylized-fact fidelity, controllable stress, microstructure realism), and
critically review evaluation, where heterogeneous protocols, leakage, and
in-distribution testing currently limit comparability. We close with a research
agenda: tail fidelity, non-stationarity guarantees, hard economic constraints,
efficiency for real-time use, privacy and governance, and standardized benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{diffusion models, score-based generative models, synthetic data,
market simulation, limit order book, risk management, derivative pricing,
portfolio optimization, stress testing}

\section{Introduction}
Generative modeling is now central to quantitative finance, for structural rather
than fashionable reasons. Financial data are \emph{scarce} in the regimes that
matter most---crises, regime transitions, and rare events are by definition
under-represented in any historical sample. They are \emph{sensitive}, since
client-level and proprietary records cannot be shared freely. And they are
\emph{non-stationary}, drifting with policy, liquidity, and market structure.
Synthetic data that faithfully reproduces the statistical signature of markets
relaxes all three constraints at once: it augments thin datasets, substitutes for
privacy-restricted records, and supplies controllable scenarios for backtesting
and stress testing~\cite{hounwanou2025deepgen,sattarov2023findiff}.

For roughly a decade this need was met primarily by generative adversarial
networks (GANs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs). Quant
GANs~\cite{wiese2020quantgan} showed that temporal convolutional GANs reproduce
volatility clustering and fat tails; signature-based Sig-Wasserstein
GANs~\cite{ni2021sigwgan} and structured-noise/MMD models~\cite{lu2024structnoise}
improved stability and path realism. Yet GANs remain hard to train, prone to mode
collapse, and awkward to condition, while VAEs oversmooth the heavy tails that
dominate financial risk.

\textbf{Why diffusion, why now.} Denoising diffusion probabilistic models
(DDPMs)~\cite{ho2020ddpm} and score-based models~\cite{song2019ncsn}, unified
under a stochastic differential equation (SDE) view~\cite{song2021sde}, offer a
different trade-off: stable likelihood-based training, strong mode coverage, and
native, flexible \emph{conditioning} through guidance~\cite{dhariwal2021beatgan,
ho2022cfg}. They also sit beside the objects quantitative finance already uses. A
diffusion model \emph{is} an SDE with a learned drift; sampling is the numerical
solution of a reverse-time SDE or its probability-flow ODE; and training has a
control-theoretic reading through a Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman (HJB) equation on
the log-density~\cite{berner2022optcontrol}. For a field built on It\^o calculus,
Monte Carlo, and stochastic control, this is a comfortable fit---and the reason
diffusion methods have, since roughly 2023, spread across nearly every
quantitative-finance task.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A two-axis taxonomy (method $\times$ financial task,
Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy}) and coverage map (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}) that
organize the literature. (ii) A consolidated
account of how diffusion's conditioning and SDE structure serve finance-specific
goals---risk-neutral generation, stylized-fact fidelity, controllable stress, and
microstructure realism. (iii) A critical review of evaluation and its pitfalls.
(iv) A research agenda. Prior reviews are GAN/VAE-centric or target generic time
series~\cite{yang2024tssurvey} and anomaly detection~\cite{liu2025anomalysurvey};
broad GenAI synthetic-data surveys~\cite{li2025gensynthsurvey} and tabular
surveys~\cite{challagundla2025tabsurvey} are not finance-specific. To our
knowledge this is the first survey dedicated to diffusion \emph{in finance}.
Section~\ref{sec:bg} reviews fundamentals; Sections~\ref{sec:synth}--\ref{sec:port}
survey applications; Section~\ref{sec:emerging} covers emerging and cross-domain
uses; Section~\ref{sec:themes} synthesizes cross-cutting themes;
Section~\ref{sec:eval} treats evaluation; and Section~\ref{sec:open} discusses
open problems.

\textbf{Scope.} We focus on diffusion and score-based models---DDPMs, continuous
score-SDEs, and their conditional, latent, fast-sampling, and structure-informed
variants---applied to financial problems. We also include the
\emph{flow-matching}, \emph{rectified-flow}, and \emph{stochastic-interpolant}
generalization (\S\ref{sec:bg}), of which diffusion is the Gaussian special case,
but treat classical normalizing flows as a related baseline. We include the most
relevant non-diffusion baselines (notably GANs and VAEs) for context but do not
survey them exhaustively, and we treat cross-domain backbones only insofar as they
are adapted to finance. Within each financial task we organize work by method. We
prioritize peer-reviewed and widely cited results, while noting that much of this
literature is recent and preprint-stage, so quantitative claims should be read as
indicative.

\section{Background}\label{sec:bg}
This section gives the minimum machinery to read the survey and, more importantly,
draws the bridge to financial mathematics.

\textbf{Forward and reverse processes.} A diffusion model defines a \emph{forward}
process that corrupts data $x_0\sim p_{\text{data}}$ into noise and learns a
\emph{reverse} process that reconstructs data from noise. In
DDPM~\cite{ho2020ddpm}, the forward chain adds Gaussian noise,
$q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(x_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\beta_t I)$, with the
closed form $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$. A
network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ is trained with the denoising objective
$\mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon,t}\,\lVert\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\rVert^2$, an
idea rooted in non-equilibrium thermodynamics~\cite{sohldickstein2015}.
Score-based models~\cite{song2019ncsn} instead learn the score
$\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ by denoising score matching across noise scales; predicting
noise and predicting the score are equivalent up to scaling. The simple objective
is a reweighting of the variational bound on $\log p_\theta(x_0)$, so training is
likelihood-based and stable---a sharp contrast with the adversarial min--max of
GANs, and a practical reason diffusion has displaced them in finance, where
training instability and mode collapse directly translate into missing market
regimes. Conditioning on covariates $c$ (a regime label, a volatility target, a
history) is handled by learning $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c)$ and, at sampling time,
combining conditional and unconditional predictions via classifier-free
guidance~\cite{ho2022cfg},
$\tilde\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)+w\,[\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c)-
\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)]$, with guidance weight $w$ trading fidelity to
$c$ against diversity---the knob that makes ``generate a crash of intensity $c$''
operational.

\textbf{The SDE unification.} \citet{song2021sde} unify both under an It\^o SDE
$dx=f(x,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW_t$ whose time reversal,
$dx=[f(x,t)-g(t)^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x)]\,dt+g(t)\,d\bar W_t$, is itself driven by
the score, with a deterministic probability-flow ODE of identical marginals.
\emph{This is the heart of the finance connection}: asset-price models are SDEs,
so a diffusion generator is a learned, data-driven SDE, and sampling is the
reverse-time simulation quants already perform---now with a neural drift. The
correspondence is operational, not merely aesthetic: it tells us where financial
structure can be injected (the drift $f$, the diffusion $g$, the noise schedule,
or the prior), how to reason about a change of measure (a drift shift, hence a
score shift; \S\ref{sec:price}), and why control-theoretic tools apply---the
training objective satisfies an HJB equation on the evolving
log-density~\cite{berner2022optcontrol}, connecting generation to the
optimal-investment and hedging problems of continuous-time finance. The
probability-flow ODE additionally yields exact likelihoods, useful for anomaly
scoring and model comparison.

\textbf{Discrete vs.\ continuous, and which to use.} The discrete DDPM and the
continuous score-SDE are two views of one object, but the choice has practical
consequences in finance. The discrete formulation is convenient for fixed-length
tabular records and short return windows~\cite{kotelnikov2023tabddpm,
sattarov2023findiff}; the continuous SDE view is natural when the target is itself
a process---asset paths, yield-curve dynamics, or order flow---because it exposes
the drift and diffusion terms where economic structure (mean reversion, jumps, the
risk-neutral shift) is most naturally imposed~\cite{kim2025gbmdiff,
gao2025sdepaths,tiwari2026riskneutral}. Few-step distillations and latent variants
apply to either and are increasingly used when sampling cost is binding. In
practice, financial models mix these ingredients freely: a latent-space score
model with a transformer backbone, classifier-free conditioning, and a
finance-specific prior is a common composite.

\textbf{From recipe to practice.} Building a financial diffusion model thus
involves a small set of recurring choices: the \emph{backbone} (Transformer for
long-range and cross-asset dependence, convolutional/U-Net for local structure,
state-space for very long sequences~\cite{alcaraz2022sssd}); the
\emph{representation} (raw series, latent code, image, or byte stream); the
\emph{conditioning signal} and how it is injected (concatenation, cross-attention,
or classifier-free guidance); the \emph{noise schedule and parameterization}; and
any \emph{structural prior} encoding economic knowledge. The same few decisions
recur across tasks, which is why methods transfer readily between, say, LOB
simulation and scenario generation once the modality is matched. This modularity
is a strength---progress in generic diffusion propagates quickly to finance---but
it also means careless transfer can import assumptions (Gaussianity, stationarity,
exchangeability) that financial data violate.

\textbf{Sampling and acceleration.} Ancestral sampling needs hundreds to thousands
of network evaluations, prohibitive for high-frequency or large-scale risk use.
Refinements add learned variances and improved schedules~\cite{nichol2021improved};
DDIM~\cite{songddim2021}, EDM~\cite{karras2022edm}, consistency
models~\cite{song2023consistency}, and flow matching~\cite{lipman2023flow} push
toward few-step generation. Sampling cost recurs as a binding constraint below.

\textbf{Flows and interpolants: the broader family.} Flow matching is also the
entry point to a \emph{generalization} of diffusion that we treat as in scope.
Rectified flow~\cite{liu2023rectifiedflow} learns a neural ODE transporting noise
to data along near-straight paths by least-squares velocity regression, and
stochastic interpolants~\cite{albergo2023interpolants} give a unifying framework in
which a tunable interpolation between any two densities yields both a
probability-flow ODE and a family of SDEs, with classical diffusion recovered as
the Gaussian special case. For finance this matters because arbitrary base
distributions and interpolation schedules let priors be built into the
\emph{path}, and the ODE form supports few-step sampling where latency binds. We
therefore include flow-matching/rectified-flow/interpolant methods alongside
DDPM and score-SDE, while treating \emph{classical} normalizing flows (invertible
exact-likelihood maps) as a related baseline rather than as diffusion.

\textbf{Conditioning and backbones.} Finance rarely wants unconditional samples;
it wants samples \emph{given} a regime, a volatility target, or a history.
Classifier~\cite{dhariwal2021beatgan} and classifier-free~\cite{ho2022cfg}
guidance steer generation; latent diffusion~\cite{rombach2022ldm} improves
efficiency. Cross-domain backbones recur as finance models and baselines:
TimeGrad~\cite{rasul2021timegrad}, CSDI~\cite{tashiro2021csdi},
SSSD~\cite{alcaraz2022sssd}, Diffusion-TS~\cite{yuan2024diffusionts}, and
TSDiff~\cite{kollovieh2023tsdiff} for sequences; TabDDPM~\cite{kotelnikov2023tabddpm}
and TabSyn~\cite{zhang2024tabsyn} for tables; DiT~\cite{peebles2022dit} for
transformer backbones; and Diffuser~\cite{janner2022diffuser}/Decision
Diffuser~\cite{ajay2022decisiondiffuser} (surveyed by~\citet{zhu2023diff4rl}) for
decision-making-as-generation. The flow/interpolant family supplies its own
sequence backbones---FlowTS~\cite{hu2024flowts} and TimeFlow~\cite{he2025timeflow}
for generation, and interpolant-based probabilistic
forecasting~\cite{chen2024siforecasting,chen2024recurrentinterpolants}---and
RecTable~\cite{fuchi2025rectable} for tables.

\textbf{Why finance is hard.} Generic successes do not transfer automatically.
Returns exhibit heavy (power-law) tails, volatility clustering, the leverage
effect, and weak return autocorrelation but strong autocorrelation of absolute
returns; the distribution drifts; the signal-to-noise ratio is low; LOB data are
discrete, bursty, and rule-governed; and pricing requires no-arbitrage and the
correct change of measure. These tensions motivate \emph{structure-informed}
designs---GBM-based forward processes~\cite{kim2025gbmdiff}, factor
structure~\cite{chen2025difffactor}, arbitrage penalties~\cite{jin2025ivsurface},
and risk-neutral drift corrections---that recur throughout.

\section{Synthetic Data and Time-Series Generation}\label{sec:synth}
The largest cluster targets data that passes for real. Two design philosophies
recur. The first \emph{reshapes} data so off-the-shelf diffusion applies:
\citet{takahashi2024synthfts} apply a wavelet transform mapping price, volume, and
spread into image-like representations, train a DDPM, and invert it, recovering
fat tails and clustering. The second \emph{bakes in} financial structure:
\citet{kim2025gbmdiff} replace Gaussian corruption with a geometric-Brownian-motion
forward process, improving heavy-tail and clustering fidelity. The lesson is that
inductive bias---priors in the noise process or representation---outperforms
architecture tuning alone. The fidelity bar is unusually demanding: a financial
generator must reproduce a battery of \emph{stylized facts} simultaneously---fat
(power-law) tails, volatility clustering, the leverage effect, weak return
autocorrelation alongside strong autocorrelation of absolute returns, and
gain/loss asymmetry---none of which a low reconstruction error guarantees. Score
networks for these tasks typically use Transformer, U-Net, or convolutional
backbones with explicit temporal and positional encodings and diffusion-step
embeddings to capture non-stationary temporal dependence; the multivariate case
additionally demands faithful cross-sectional dependence, which motivates the
factor- and correlation-structured designs that recur in
\S\ref{sec:risk}--\ref{sec:port}.

The data modalities span a wide range, and the appropriate machinery differs by
modality. Low-frequency univariate and panel returns are the most studied setting
and the natural home of stylized-fact evaluation~\cite{takahashi2024synthfts,
chen2025difffactor}. High-frequency and microstructure data (\S\ref{sec:lob})
are discrete and event-driven, demanding event- or byte-level
models~\cite{zheng2024lobdif,li2025bytegen}. Mixed-type tabular records---credit,
fraud, fund holdings---require combined continuous/categorical
diffusion~\cite{kotelnikov2023tabddpm,sattarov2023findiff}. And structured objects
such as implied-volatility surfaces or yield curves are best modeled with the
relevant no-arbitrage constraints built in~\cite{jin2025ivsurface,
fukunishi2025termstructure}. A practical implication is that ``diffusion for
finance'' is not one method but a family, selected and specialized per modality;
the cross-domain backbones of \S\ref{sec:bg} supply the starting points.

\textbf{High-dimensional and conditional generation.} Portfolio and risk
applications need realistic \emph{dependence}. \citet{chen2025difffactor} embed a
latent factor structure into diffusion, generating high-dimensional returns that
respect a factor covariance, with theoretical guarantees and small-sample gains.
Conditioning turns a generator into a scenario engine:
CoFinDiff~\cite{tanaka2025cofindiff} controls trend and realized volatility with
lower control error and more diversity than GAN/VAE baselines, and downstream
hedging gains. Fidelity is ultimately judged by use: Fiaingen~\cite{rozanec2025fiaingen}
reports train-on-synthetic/test-on-real near parity with real data, and
comparative studies position diffusion against TimeGAN/VAE as a privacy-preserving
substitute~\cite{hounwanou2025deepgen}; general style-guided TS
diffusion~\cite{sun2025dsdiffusion} supplies further backbone machinery.

\textbf{Tabular data, fraud, and privacy.} Much financial data is mixed-type
tabular. TabDDPM~\cite{kotelnikov2023tabddpm}, latent-space
TabSyn~\cite{zhang2024tabsyn}, and its rectified-flow analogue
RecTable~\cite{fuchi2025rectable} define the general state of the art;
FinDiff~\cite{sattarov2023findiff}, presented at ICAIF, specializes it to
financial tables (credit, expenditure, fund composition) with strong fidelity,
utility, and privacy versus TVAE/CTGAN/TabDDPM. Two extensions address the field's
defining difficulty---class imbalance: Imb-FinDiff~\cite{sattarov2024imbfindiff}
adds a joint denoising-plus-label objective for minority (fraud) synthesis, and
EntTabDiff~\cite{liu2024enttabdiff} conditions on \emph{entities} to improve
fidelity and reduce entity-level leakage. For fraud, EmDT~\cite{kuo2026emdt} trains
per-cluster diffusion transformers and latent flow-diffusion~\cite{ihsan2025latentflow}
targets minority oversampling. Privacy is moving from proxies to formal
guarantees: DP-FinDiff~\cite{sattarov2025dpfindiff} and federated
DP-FedTabDiff~\cite{sattarov2024dpfedtabdiff} train under differential privacy,
and~\citet{zuo2026privacyrisks} benchmark the privacy--utility frontier. Actuarial
use is adjacent (survival-data diffusion~\cite{brockschmidt2025survdiff}). The
non-diffusion frontier (adversarial synthetic data~\cite{xia2026bayesrobust})
remains the comparison point.

\textbf{Relation to GANs, VAEs, and flows.} It is worth stating the comparison
plainly, since these baselines recur throughout. GANs---Quant
GANs~\cite{wiese2020quantgan}, Sig-Wasserstein GANs~\cite{ni2021sigwgan}, and
signature-MMD models~\cite{lu2024structnoise}---can reproduce stylized facts and
were the first deep generators to do so convincingly, but their adversarial
training is unstable and prone to mode collapse, and conditioning them on rich
controls is awkward. VAEs train stably and give latent structure but tend to
oversmooth, blunting exactly the heavy tails finance cares about. Across the
surveyed studies the reported pattern is consistent: diffusion matches or exceeds
these baselines on stylized-fact and downstream metrics while conditioning more
naturally, at the cost of slower sampling. None of this is a clean dominance
claim---cross-paper comparison is confounded by inconsistent data and metrics
(\S\ref{sec:eval})---but it explains the field's rapid migration toward diffusion.

\section{Market Microstructure and LOB Simulation}\label{sec:lob}
Simulating the LOB and its order flow is harder than low-frequency generation: the
data are high-frequency, discrete, bursty, and rule-governed, and a useful
simulator must be \emph{responsive}---reacting plausibly to an agent's own orders,
so it can serve backtesting, market-impact studies, and agent-based experiments.

\textbf{Order flow and representations.} TRADES~\cite{berti2025trades} generates
order flow with a transformer diffusion conditioned on market state, reporting
predictive-score gains; since the book reconstructs from the flow, realism
propagates. ``Painting the Market''~\cite{backhouse2025painting} renders the LOB
as an image and applies inpainting, generating long horizons in parallel and
avoiding the error accumulation of autoregressive simulators, scoring well on
LOB-Bench. ByteGen~\cite{li2025bytegen} forgoes tokenization, generating events
directly in byte space with a hybrid Mamba--Transformer.

\textbf{Volume, counterfactuals, and control.} Beyond price levels, the volume
profile governs liquidity and impact: DiffVolume~\cite{wang2025diffvolume}
generates volume snapshots with \emph{counterfactual} control, and
DiffLOB~\cite{wang2026difflob} produces regime-conditioned counterfactual LOB
trajectories---supporting microstructure-level what-if stress.
DigMA~\cite{huang2024digma} pairs a conditional diffusion of the market state with
a financially informed meta-agent for controllable, responsive order generation.
Event-stream models (LOBDIF~\cite{zheng2024lobdif}) predict the joint timing and
type of events with skip-step sampling for latency. Diffusion also serves upstream
as a market-series \emph{denoiser}~\cite{wang2024ftsdenoiser}, and lower-frequency
dynamics simulation yields portfolio-ready covariances~\cite{lesniewski2024beyondmc}.
Interpretable evaluation uses optimal transport to test power-law
reproduction~\cite{hashimoto2025otpowerlaw}.

The defining requirement that separates microstructure simulation from mere
sequence generation is \emph{responsiveness}: under a strategy backtest or
market-impact study the simulated book must react plausibly to the evaluated
agent's own orders, not merely replay history. Conditioning the generator on the
evolving market state---and, in DigMA~\cite{huang2024digma}, on an explicit
agent---is the route to this, and counterfactual generation
\cite{wang2025diffvolume,wang2026difflob} is its controllable form. Two
constraints temper progress: latency (thousand-step sampling is infeasible at
high frequency, motivating the skip-step scheme of LOBDIF~\cite{zheng2024lobdif}
and few-step samplers generally) and the discrete, rule-governed nature of order
events, which byte- and event-level models~\cite{li2025bytegen,zheng2024lobdif}
address more directly than continuous-image framings.

This work sits in a longer arc of market simulation that moved from hand-built
agent-based and stochastic point-process models, through GAN-based generators, to
the current diffusion frontier, which packages stable training, broad
distributional coverage, and flexible conditioning. Diffusion simulators are
increasingly positioned as substrates for \emph{agent-based}
experiments~\cite{berti2025trades,huang2024digma}: a calibrated, responsive
generator lets researchers inject hypothetical agents or shocks and observe
plausible market reactions, which is precisely what counterfactual and
regime-conditioned designs~\cite{wang2025diffvolume,wang2026difflob} enable. The
open evaluation question---how to certify that a simulator's \emph{reaction} to an
unseen agent is realistic, not merely that its unconditional samples match
history---remains the key obstacle to using these simulators for high-stakes
strategy validation.

\section{Risk, Scenario Generation, and Stress Testing}\label{sec:risk}
Controllability pays off most in risk, where the goal is to populate \emph{tails}
and \emph{what-ifs} absent from history. Guidance~\cite{dhariwal2021beatgan,
ho2022cfg} yields samples \emph{given} a crash intensity or regime; the same
heavy-tail fidelity that aids generation makes the resulting Value-at-Risk and
Expected-Shortfall estimates credible---a generator that thins tails understates
risk. Methodologically, a trained generator is used as a Monte Carlo engine:
risk measures (VaR, ES/CVaR) and their sensitivities are estimated from sampled
scenarios, optionally conditioned on a stressed regime, so the quality of the
tail directly determines the quality of the capital number. This makes the
stylized-fact and tail-fidelity evaluation of \S\ref{sec:eval} not a cosmetic
check but a precondition for trustworthy risk use, and it is why several recent
methods add explicitly tail-weighted training objectives or regime
conditioning~\cite{alzahrani2025marcd,wang2025neuralllevy} rather than relying on
a generator tuned for average-case realism. Scenario generation is most useful wired into a decision:
diffusion-augmented RL~\cite{choudhary2025diffaugrl} trains robust policies against
generated stress, and MARCD~\cite{alzahrani2025marcd} couples a regime-aligned
diffusion generator (a hidden Markov model) to a CVaR-constrained allocator with a
tail-weighted objective, reporting large drawdown reductions. Structure-aware
generation applies stress coherently: factor-structured
diffusion~\cite{chen2025difffactor} shocks factors while preserving dependence;
compressed-sensing-accelerated diffusion in a low-dimensional macro
space~\cite{guo2025compressedsensing} reproduces left-tail quantiles for
backtesting; diffusion scenario trees~\cite{zarifis2025scenariotree} feed
multistage stochastic optimization; and neural L\'evy
jump-diffusion~\cite{wang2025neuralllevy} targets jump-dominated tails. Many
techniques transfer from energy scenario
generation~\cite{li2023diffcharge,wang2025dayaheadenergy}. Regulated stress testing
could use richer, internally consistent scenario sets, but a scenario generator
used for capital must itself be validated and auditable
(Section~\ref{sec:open})---in our view the main barrier between these results and
regulated deployment.

\section{Pricing, Hedging, and Calibration}\label{sec:price}
Pricing imposes the hardest economic constraints: no-arbitrage and the change of
measure from the physical ($\mathbb{P}$) to the risk-neutral ($\mathbb{Q}$) world.
A change of measure shifts the SDE drift~\cite{song2021sde}, which in
score/$\epsilon$ terms is an additive correction to the reverse dynamics;
\citet{tiwari2026riskneutral} realize exactly this as a score-function shift
enforcing the martingale condition, making the measure change explicit rather than
approximate (cf.\ the built-in risk-neutral transition of Quant
GANs~\cite{wiese2020quantgan}). \citet{zhao2025exotic} price exotic and
path-dependent options (European, arithmetic Asian) with conditional diffusion,
honestly reporting degraded accuracy on \emph{extreme} scenarios---the crux for
risk-sensitive products. \citet{jin2025ivsurface} generate arbitrage-aware
implied-volatility surfaces one day ahead by penalizing arbitrage violations in
training, beating GAN-based surface models.

\textbf{Fixed income and foundations.} Diffusion has reached rates:
\citet{fukunishi2025termstructure} generate arbitrage-aware yield-curve term
structures with macroeconomic conditioning, and~\citet{zhang2026tfcodit} synthesize
language-conditioned Treasury-futures series. More foundationally,
\citet{gao2025sdepaths} generate SDE solution paths \emph{without} known drift and
diffusion coefficients---the calibration setting when the model is unknown---and
the HJB view~\cite{berner2022optcontrol} links diffusion to the optimal-investment
and hedging equations. Deep hedging~\cite{buehler2019deephedging} is the downstream
decision these generators serve: better training distributions yield better learned
hedges, as CoFinDiff~\cite{tanaka2025cofindiff} demonstrates. Neural PDE solvers
for (rough) volatility pricing~\cite{papapantoleon2024deepgradflow} are a
complementary, non-generative route. Calibration---fitting a model to observed market prices---reappears throughout:
generating arbitrage-consistent surfaces~\cite{jin2025ivsurface} or term
structures~\cite{fukunishi2025termstructure} is calibration in distributional
form, and learning unknown SDE coefficients from data~\cite{gao2025sdepaths} is
calibration when the model class itself is uncertain. Hedging connects pricing to
decisions: with a faithful $\mathbb{Q}$-generator, sensitivities (Greeks) and
hedge ratios can be estimated by simulation, and deep-hedging
policies~\cite{buehler2019deephedging} can be trained on diffusion-generated paths
that include frictions and jumps. The unresolved frontier is enforcing
\emph{exact} no-arbitrage and the martingale property by construction---via
constrained sampling, projection, or hard-constrained guidance---rather than
penalizing their violation, while preserving tail accuracy.

\section{Forecasting and Portfolio Construction}\label{sec:port}
Diffusion models are \emph{probabilistic} forecasters---they produce predictive
distributions, which is what portfolio construction and risk budgeting consume.
TimeGrad~\cite{rasul2021timegrad} is the standard baseline; in finance,
Diffolio~\cite{cho2025diffolio} adds hierarchical (asset- and market-level)
attention and a correlation-guided regularizer, so the predictive distribution
respects cross-sectional dependence, improving both accuracy and downstream Sharpe
and certainty-equivalent. Allocation follows two routes. \emph{Predict-then-optimize}:
factor-conditioned diffusion learns the cross-sectional return distribution and
optimizes mean-variance/mean-CVaR portfolios~\cite{gao2025factorcond}, with a
bias--variance analysis of the conditioning-factor count~\cite{bagchi2026factordim}.
\emph{Direct} policy learning: model-free dynamic selection beats
Markowitz~\cite{aghapour2025dynport}, building on decision-as-generation
\cite{janner2022diffuser,ajay2022decisiondiffuser}; the flow-matching form of this
idea, FlowHFT~\cite{li2025flowhft}, learns an imitation-learning trading policy for
high-frequency trading that adapts across market regimes. Relational and augmentation
variants include DiffSTOCK~\cite{daiya2024diffstock} (conditioning on indicators
and inter-stock relations) and DiffsFormer~\cite{gao2024diffsformer} (factor
augmentation); chart-image forecasting~\cite{lee2025charts} illustrates the role of
representation. A third, indirect route is \emph{augmentation}: synthetic paths
enlarge thin training sets for downstream predictors and reinforcement-learning
agents~\cite{gao2024diffsformer,lu2024structnoise}, and the generate-then-optimize
pipeline of \S\ref{sec:risk}~\cite{choudhary2025diffaugrl,alzahrani2025marcd}
closes the loop by optimizing the allocation directly against generated scenarios.
Across all three routes the strongest results couple a financial inductive bias to
the generator rather than relying on a generic backbone. The recurring caveat is
evaluation: portfolio metrics are especially vulnerable to look-ahead bias and to
overfitting a single backtest, a theme we return to next.

\section{Emerging and Cross-Domain Applications}\label{sec:emerging}
Beyond the five core tasks, several adjacent applications are maturing.
\emph{Fixed income} has its first generative treatments---arbitrage-aware term
structures~\cite{fukunishi2025termstructure} and Treasury-futures
synthesis~\cite{zhang2026tfcodit}---extending diffusion from equities to rates.
\emph{Insurance and actuarial} use is nascent but methodologically close:
diffusion for survival (time-to-event) data~\cite{brockschmidt2025survdiff} maps
onto mortality and claims modeling. \emph{Macro-conditioned} generation links
return distributions to economic state, a capability shared by factor-conditioned
models~\cite{gao2025factorcond} and macro-aware robust-trading
generators~\cite{xia2026bayesrobust}. Finally, much methodology transfers from
\emph{energy} markets, where conditional diffusion for scenario generation is
comparatively mature~\cite{li2023diffcharge,wang2025dayaheadenergy}; these
electricity- and mobility-scenario techniques are routinely validated before
adaptation to financial risk, and the multistage-stochastic-optimization framing
of scenario trees~\cite{zarifis2025scenariotree} applies equally to energy
arbitrage and to financial asset--liability management. The pattern is consistent:
a technique proven on a structurally similar non-financial problem is imported and
specialized with a financial prior.

\section{Cross-Cutting Themes}\label{sec:themes}
Read across tasks, four threads organize the field. \textbf{Conditioning is the
norm, not the exception.} The most recent and most finance-specific
work---controllable generation~\cite{tanaka2025cofindiff}, counterfactual
microstructure~\cite{wang2025diffvolume,wang2026difflob}, stress
scenarios~\cite{choudhary2025diffaugrl,alzahrani2025marcd}, and
forecast-conditioned allocation~\cite{cho2025diffolio,gao2025factorcond}---is
conditional, because controllability is what finance actually wants from a
generator. \textbf{Inductive bias beats architecture tuning.} The wins come from
encoding financial structure---GBM noising~\cite{kim2025gbmdiff}, factor
covariance~\cite{chen2025difffactor}, arbitrage penalties~\cite{jin2025ivsurface},
risk-neutral drift shifts~\cite{tiwari2026riskneutral}, correlation
regularizers~\cite{cho2025diffolio}---into the noise process, the prior, or the
loss. \textbf{Representation is a design lever.} Wavelet-to-image
\cite{takahashi2024synthfts}, LOB-as-image~\cite{backhouse2025painting},
byte-space~\cite{li2025bytegen}, latent~\cite{rombach2022ldm,zhang2024tabsyn}, and
chart-image~\cite{lee2025charts} framings repeatedly unlock generic diffusion
machinery for financial objects. \textbf{Generation increasingly serves a
decision.} The frontier couples the generator to control---deep
hedging~\cite{buehler2019deephedging,tanaka2025cofindiff}, CVaR
allocation~\cite{alzahrani2025marcd}, multistage stochastic
optimization~\cite{zarifis2025scenariotree}, and the diffusion-as-policy
line~\cite{janner2022diffuser,ajay2022decisiondiffuser}---rather than treating
realistic samples as an end in themselves.

\textbf{Deployment considerations.} Three practical constraints cut across all of
the above. \emph{Latency}: real-time and high-frequency use cannot afford
many-step sampling, so few-step methods~\cite{songddim2021,song2023consistency,
lipman2023flow} and skip-step schemes~\cite{zheng2024lobdif} are load-bearing, not
optional. \emph{Data governance}: when synthetic data substitutes for sensitive
records, differential privacy and membership-inference resistance become hard
requirements~\cite{sattarov2025dpfindiff,sattarov2024dpfedtabdiff,
zuo2026privacyrisks}. \emph{Validation}: any generator feeding capital, valuation,
or surveillance inherits model-risk-management duties---a generator used to set a
capital number must itself be validated, monitored, and auditable.

\section{Evaluation, Benchmarks, Reproducibility}\label{sec:eval}
Evaluation is the weakest link, and matters more in finance than in vision: a
generator that looks good by a generic metric can misprice tail risk or leak
look-ahead information. \emph{Stylized-fact} fidelity (excess kurtosis, the Hill
tail index, autocorrelation of returns vs.\ absolute returns) is the first-line
test~\cite{takahashi2024synthfts,kim2025gbmdiff}, with optimal-transport distances
a more interpretable, mechanism-attributing alternative for market
simulators~\cite{hashimoto2025otpowerlaw}; these checks are necessary but largely
marginal. \emph{Distributional/downstream} metrics probe joint and task-relevant
structure: discriminative/predictive scores~\cite{berti2025trades} and
train-on-synthetic/test-on-real~\cite{rozanec2025fiaingen}, the operational
standard for replacing real data. Microstructure has
LOB-Bench~\cite{backhouse2025painting}, though \emph{responsiveness} (impact
realism under the simulated agent's own actions) remains non-standardized.
\emph{Privacy} is first-class when synthetic data substitutes for sensitive
records~\cite{sattarov2023findiff,sattarov2025dpfindiff,zuo2026privacyrisks}.
It is useful to view these as four metric families---marginal (stylized facts),
joint/distributional (discriminative scores), task (downstream utility), and
privacy---each guarding against a different failure: a model can ace marginals yet
fail joint dependence, or attain high utility yet leak membership. A credible
evaluation reports at least one from each family; reporting only marginals, still
common, is the weakest acceptable bar and the easiest to game.

Three pitfalls undermine comparability. (i) \emph{Heterogeneous data and metrics}:
papers use different assets, periods, and scores, so reported superiority rarely
transfers. (ii) \emph{Leakage and look-ahead bias}: full-sample normalization or
conditioning on information unavailable at decision time inflates results,
especially in forecasting and portfolio studies. (iii) \emph{In-distribution
evaluation}: testing on the training regime hides the non-stationarity failure
that matters in deployment. We recommend chronological splits, regime-shifted test
sets, fixed public benchmarks, and released code; the absence of a shared,
finance-specific benchmark suite is the single biggest obstacle to measurable
progress.

\textbf{A recommended reporting protocol.} Concretely, we suggest each study
report: (i) the exact data source, asset universe, and date range; (ii) a
\emph{chronological} train/test split with an explicit out-of-regime test period
(e.g.\ a held-out crisis); (iii) at least one stylized-fact metric, one
discriminative/predictive score, and one downstream-task (train-on-synthetic,
test-on-real) metric, since each probes a different failure mode; (iv) for
private data, a stated privacy notion and budget rather than an empirical proxy
alone; and (v) released code and preprocessing. None of these is universal today,
which is why headline claims of superiority so rarely reproduce across papers.

\section{Open Problems}\label{sec:open}
\textbf{Tail and extreme-event fidelity.} Decision-relevant mass lives in the
tails, yet strong results degrade there~\cite{zhao2025exotic}, and risk numbers
inherit any tail thinning. Objectives and noise schedules that explicitly target
tail accuracy, and rare-event conditioning, are open; structure-informed
designs~\cite{kim2025gbmdiff,chen2025difffactor} help but do not solve it.
\textbf{Non-stationarity.} Out-of-regime generalization and guarantees under shift
are largely unaddressed; existing guarantees are narrow~\cite{chen2025difffactor}.
\textbf{Hard economic constraints.} Enforcing exact no-arbitrage and the
$\mathbb{Q}$-martingale property by construction (not penalty~\cite{jin2025ivsurface})
is a prerequisite for front-office adoption. \textbf{Efficiency.} Few-step
sampling~\cite{songddim2021,song2023consistency,lipman2023flow} for real-time/HFT
is underexplored on heavy-tailed targets and is essential for responsive
simulation. \textbf{Decision integration.} Coupling generation to
control~\cite{berner2022optcontrol,gao2025sdepaths,choudhary2025diffaugrl} is
promising but immature. \textbf{Governance.} Model risk, auditability, and formal
privacy~\cite{sattarov2025dpfindiff} gate regulated use. \textbf{Causality and counterfactuals.} Most models are correlational generators;
counterfactual microstructure~\cite{wang2026difflob,wang2025diffvolume} and
controllable agents~\cite{huang2024digma} gesture toward intervention, but
principled causal diffusion---answering ``what if this policy or shock
occurred''---is largely unexplored and is precisely what stress testing requires.
\textbf{Theory.} Approximation and sample-complexity guarantees remain narrow:
factor-structure results~\cite{chen2025difffactor} and the control-theoretic
view~\cite{berner2022optcontrol} are starting points, but error bounds under
non-stationarity and for heavy-tailed targets are open. \textbf{Benchmarks.} A
shared finance-specific suite---returns, LOB~\cite{backhouse2025painting},
tabular~\cite{sattarov2023findiff}, and risk---with chronological, regime-aware
splits would do for this field what community benchmarks did for vision diffusion.

\section{Conclusion}
Diffusion models have earned a permanent place in the quantitative-finance
toolkit, mapping their stable training, mode coverage, conditioning, and SDE
structure onto finance's needs across data generation, microstructure simulation,
risk, pricing, and portfolio decisions. The evidence is encouraging, but the
field's progress is harder to measure than to produce: tail fidelity degrades
where finance cares most, evaluation is fragmented and frequently in-distribution,
economic constraints are penalized rather than enforced, sampling is too slow for
real-time use, and governance for regulated deployment is barely begun. The next
phase depends less on new architectures than on discipline: finance-specific
benchmarks with chronological and regime-aware splits, tail- and arbitrage-aware
objectives, formal privacy and model-risk treatment, and tighter coupling of
generation to the decision it serves.

\textbf{Scope and limitations of this survey.} We have emphasized breadth across
financial tasks over exhaustive coverage of any one; some fast-moving
sub-areas---multimodal (news/price) generation, graph-based systemic-risk
modeling, and crypto-specific work---are touched only briefly and merit dedicated
treatment. Because the literature is recent and largely preprint-stage, reported
results should be read as indicative rather than settled, which reinforces our call
for shared benchmarks. We expect the method $\times$ task map of
Figures~\ref{fig:taxonomy}--\ref{fig:coverage} to fill in rapidly, particularly in the under-served
cells of hard-constrained pricing and tail-faithful risk.
